02 / THE SCOUTING REPORT

Which one is better?

For sleep and body-clock alignment, standard time gets the medical recommendation. For light after work, daylight time has the obvious appeal.

THE HEALTH PICK

Permanent standard time

The upside: Earlier morning light supports your body clock. Winter mornings stay as they are now.

The cost: Summer sunsets move an hour earlier. That’s one less hour of evening daylight for the park, patio, or Little League.

Keep the winter mornings. Give up the late summer finish.

THE EVENING PICK

Permanent daylight time

The upside: Keep today’s summer evenings. Add an hour of light to winter afternoons and evenings.

The cost: Winter sunrises move an hour later. That hits early commuters and school schedules, especially farther west in a time zone.

Keep the late finish. Pay for it the next morning.

What if we just keep switching?

You keep today’s summer evenings and winter mornings, plus the twice-yearly disruption. The spring shift temporarily cuts sleep; medical groups favor ending the changes and adopting standard time year-round.

The evening coalition

Trump and Sen. Rick Scott favor permanent DST. Supporters point to more light for family time, shopping, and recreation after work.

The morning resistance

Sen. Tom Cotton has opposed permanent DST, citing dark school and work commutes. Sleep specialists favor standard time. Geography and daily schedules complicate the usual party lines.

Both parties split on the House vote. A majority for ending the switch is not automatically a majority for the same clock.

There’s also a standard-time bill

The Sunshine for Our Kids Act (H.R. 9638), introduced by Reps. Mary Gay Scanlon (D–PA) and Pat Harrigan (R–NC), would make standard time the default while letting states choose permanent daylight time. So “no DST” does have a bipartisan vehicle, too.

The short version of the long fight
  1. 1974A year-round DST experiment meets a winter-morning backlash. Congress restores winter standard time that October.
  2. 2022The Senate passes an earlier Sunshine Protection Act. The House doesn’t finish the job.
  3. 2026The House passes H.R. 139. It still needs the Senate: votes from different Congresses don’t combine.

Like the 2019 Nats in Houston: winning the last game doesn’t win you the next one.

Can my state pick its own clock?

Federal law already lets states opt out of DST and stay on standard time. Hawaii and most of Arizona do; the Navajo Nation observes DST. Year-round daylight time requires a change in federal law. Several states have passed measures contingent on federal permission, sometimes with additional conditions.
