A daylight saving time explainer, scored like the 2019 Washington Nationals

Everybody wants to stop changing the clocks. The fight is over which clock.

In July the House voted to make daylight time permanent. The Senate hasn't moved. Here's what "permanent daylight time" and "permanent standard time" actually mean, the real case for and against each, and why an idea almost everyone likes keeps stranding runners in scoring position.

May 23, 2019

World Series champions, Oct. 30

The 19–31 part: the switch itself

On May 23, 2019 the Nationals were 19–31, twelve games under .500, and the manager's whole plan was to go 1–0 that day. Nobody argued about whether the start had been bad. They argued about what to do next.

The clock debate has the same shape. The part nearly everyone agrees is bad is the switch: twice a year, every clock in the country jumps an hour and nothing good happens that week. Studies around the spring change find small but measurable bumps in car crashes, heart attacks, and workplace injuries in the days after. The fall change drops darkness on the evening commute overnight. Polls for years have found a majority wants to stop switching.

Then it falls apart, because "stop switching" means two opposite things, and each one costs somebody an hour of light they care about.

Two ways to lock the clock

Permanent daylight time means you never fall back. Clocks stay an hour ahead of the sun all year. Your summers don't change. Your winters get later sunrises and later sunsets. This is what the Sunshine Protection Act does.

Permanent standard time means you never spring forward. Clocks stay matched to the sun all year. Your winters don't change. Your summers get earlier sunrises and earlier sunsets. States can already choose this on their own; Arizona and Hawaii did.

The total daylight is identical either way. You're only deciding which end of the day gets it. Drag the date and watch the three options separate.

Notice two things. In winter, "keep switching" is the same as permanent standard time, and in summer it's the same as permanent daylight time; the status quo is just both options stapled together. And the pain moves west: Indianapolis and Sioux Falls sit on the western edges of their time zones, so permanent daylight time pushes their December sunrises toward nine o'clock. That's the Senate's problem in one slider.

Scouting reports

Permanent daylight time

Clocks an hour ahead of the sun, all year. The Sunshine Protection Act.

The case for

  • Light when people are awake to use it. An hour after work and school in winter, instead of an hour before most people get up.
  • Evening activity. Retail, restaurants, golf, youth sports, and outdoor recreation all push for it. Some research ties evening light to less street crime and more physical activity.
  • It's the bill that exists. It passed the Senate by unanimous consent in 2022, the House 308–117 in July 2026, and the president is for it. Roughly 19 states have passed laws that take effect the moment Congress allows it.

The case against

  • Dark winter mornings. Sunrise after 8 a.m. for weeks across much of the country, and after 9 on the western edges of time zones. Kids wait for buses in the dark.
  • We tried it. In January 1974, during the energy crisis, Congress made daylight time permanent. Approval collapsed within months after reports of children struck in dark mornings, and it was repealed by October, less than a year in.
  • Sleep science is against it. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine, the AMA, and most chronobiologists say morning light anchors the body clock. Permanent daylight time is a year-round hour of social jet lag.

Permanent standard time

Clocks matched to the sun, all year. What Arizona and Hawaii already do.

The case for

  • It matches the sun. Solar noon lands near clock noon. Morning light is what sets sleep timing, mood, and alertness, which is why the medical groups endorse this option.
  • Safer winter mornings. Commuters and schoolchildren keep the daylight they have now, with no 1974 repeat.
  • No act of Congress needed. Federal law already lets a state opt out of daylight time. Any legislature could do this next session.

The case against

  • Summer evenings end an hour earlier. A late-June sunset in Washington moves from about 8:37 to about 7:37. Sunrise moves to about 4:45, light almost everyone sleeps through.
  • Less popular when it's real. Business and recreation groups oppose it, and despite the option being available for decades, almost no state has taken it. Polls are split and shift with the question's wording.
  • No vehicle. There is no permanent-standard-time bill moving in Congress. Its path is state by state, or by amending the bill the House already passed.

Keep switching

The default. What happens if the Senate does nothing.

You get the summer evenings and the winter mornings, and you pay for them with two disruptive weeks a year. It's the least popular option in most polls and the one the country has actually chosen for sixty years, because every time Congress gets close to picking a side, one side's hour of light turns out to matter more than the other side expected.

The box score: politics and status

  1. 1918 to 1966

    Daylight time arrives as a wartime measure, gets repealed, and spends decades as a patchwork of local rules. The Uniform Time Act of 1966 sets national dates and lets states opt out, but only toward standard time, never toward year-round daylight time. That asymmetry is why the whole fight runs through Congress.

  2. January 1974

    Nixon signs emergency permanent daylight time to save energy. It's popular for about a month. By October Congress has repealed it.

    2019A ten-month season that ended badly. Everyone remembers it; nobody wants the sequel.

  3. 2007

    The current March-to-November schedule takes effect, stretching daylight time to eight months of the year.

  4. 2018

    Florida passes the first state "trigger" law for year-round daylight time. Marco Rubio introduces the Sunshine Protection Act. It dies in committee in 2018 and again in 2019. The 2021 version is the one that eventually passes the Senate.

    The Senate passes the bill by unanimous consent with no debate. Several senators later say they didn't realize it was happening. The House never takes it up.

    Reintroduced as H.R. 139 and S. 29. The president's position moves: in late 2024 he says Republicans will eliminate daylight time; by spring 2025 he calls it a fifty-fifty issue; then he lands on permanent daylight time. Senate Commerce holds a hearing on the idea in April.

  7. July 14, 2026

    The House passes H.R. 139, 308–117, after moving through Energy and Commerce as part of a larger motor-vehicle package. The Senate passed the bill in 2022; now the House has too, though in a different Congress, so it has to clear the Senate again.

Where it stands today, October 3, 2026

Not the bottom of the ninth. Call it the seventh. The House has passed it, and the Senate, which passed an earlier version in 2022, is where it has to clear again. Between it and the president's desk: a committee chair's calendar, a filibuster that needs 60 votes, and a majority leader from the Dakotas who has already voted no once. States that want standard time instead would have to opt out before enactment, so a Senate vote would also set off a scramble in state capitals.

If you want to watch one thing, watch whether Commerce schedules a markup. If it doesn't happen before the end of this Congress, the bill dies on January 3 and starts over.

Where the states stand

Dozens of other legislatures have debated one option or the other without passing anything. The pattern is consistent: the states lining up for daylight time are mostly southern, where winter mornings aren't that dark anyway. The loudest skeptics are northern and western, where they are.
